LED masks are sold on one number and judged on another.

The number on the box is the LED count. It is the easiest thing to increase and the least useful thing to know. The number that decides whether a mask does anything is **irradiance**, measured in milliwatts per square centimetre, and most brands never print it.

That matters more here than in most categories, because the research on red light therapy is done with laboratory devices at known outputs. A mask that looks identical but delivers a fraction of the light is not a cheaper version of the same treatment. It is a different thing in the same shape.

So this is a guide to checking, not a list of bargains. There are two masks at the end that we list, and two we looked at and decided not to.

## The only spec that decides it

Three figures matter, and they are related.

**Wavelength**, in nanometres, determines what the light can do. The best-studied are red around 630nm and near-infrared around 830nm. A mask that will not tell you its wavelengths is telling you something.

**Irradiance**, in mW/cm², is how much light actually reaches skin. This is the figure that separates a device operating in the range the research uses from one that is mostly a novelty.

**Dose**, in joules per square centimetre, is irradiance multiplied by time. Studies on skin rejuvenation span roughly 1.2 to 126 J/cm², which is a wide range, and where a device sits in it depends on both its output and how long you wear it.

Here is the part worth carrying around: **a premium mask is not dramatically brighter than a good mid-priced one.** An [Omnilux Contour publishes about 30mW/cm²](https://omniluxled.com/products/omnilux-contour-face) at 633nm and 830nm, and costs $395. That is the benchmark the expensive end is built on, and it is a number a £145 mask can match.

## Who has actually tested these

Two independent sources are worth more than every brand claim combined.

The consumer group [Which? tested ten LED masks](https://www.which.co.uk/reviews/skincare/article/best-led-masks-aFati4a4HWgm) using 3D facial scans before and after six weeks of use, with a dermatologist analysing the results. That is the closest thing to a controlled comparison a UK buyer can read.

[Lab Muffin](https://labmuffin.com/ulike-red-light-mask-overview-and-experience/), written by a cosmetic chemist, publishes per-mode irradiance figures rather than repeating manufacturer copy. Her numbers for the Ulike below are lower than that brand's own marketing, which is exactly why they are the ones to trust.

Note what is not on that list: customer reviews. A mask that feels nice and looks impressive tells you nothing about its output, and star ratings cannot measure light.

## Frequently asked

**Do LED masks work at all?** Reviews of the clinical literature support red and blue light for acne and for skin rejuvenation, using devices at known outputs. The gap between that research and a consumer mask is dose, which is why the top of this page is about irradiance rather than results.

**Is more LEDs better?** No. LED count is the cheapest number to raise and says nothing about output per square centimetre, which is what reaches skin. Two masks with the same diode count can differ several times over in irradiance.

**How often would I need to use one?** The Ulike's protocol is three sessions a week at five to sixteen minutes depending on mode. Consistency is the part people underestimate: an unused mask has an irradiance of zero regardless of what it cost.

**Is the FDA clearance meaningful in the UK?** It is not a UK approval, and it is a clearance rather than an endorsement: a 510(k) means a device is substantially equivalent to one already on the market for the stated use. It is still more than most masks in this price range have.

**Should I just buy the expensive one?** Not on the numbers here. A $395 Omnilux publishes about 30mW/cm², which the Ulike matches in Firm mode at a fraction of the price. If you want the premium brand for other reasons that is your call, but the light is not obviously the reason.
