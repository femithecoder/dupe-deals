Automatic bottle washers are among the most expensive things sold to new parents. A [Baby Brezza Bottle Washer Pro is £335](https://www.babybrezza.co.uk/products/bottle-washer-pro). The eufy equivalent we list is {{price:62}}. Neither is a cheap version of the other; they are the same tier of product at the same kind of money.

So this is not a post about finding a dupe. It is a post about whether the category is worth buying into at all, because that is the question people are really asking at three in the morning with a bottle brush in their hand.

The short answer: these machines buy back labour, not safety. Whether that trade is worth three hundred pounds depends entirely on how many bottles a day you are washing.

## What you actually have to do

Worth getting this straight first, because the marketing blurs it.

[NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/sterilising-baby-bottles/) is that feeding equipment must be sterilised until a baby is at least 12 months old. Crucially, sterilising is the second step, not the first: bottles have to be cleaned in hot soapy water with a bottle brush and rinsed in cold running water before anything is sterilised. A steriliser does not clean. It sterilises something already clean.

The NHS accepts three methods: a cold water solution for at least 30 minutes, steam sterilising by electric or microwave unit, or boiling in a pan for at least 10 minutes.

That last one matters to this comparison more than anything else on the page. **Boiling is an approved method and costs nothing.** Any machine here is competing against a pan of water, and it has to justify itself on convenience alone, because it cannot claim to be safer than a method the NHS already endorses.

## The three ways to do it

**A pan of water, £0.** Ten minutes of boiling, after washing by hand. Approved, free, and tedious, and the tedium is the entire market for everything below.

**A steriliser and dryer, around {{price:86}}.** Automates the sterilising and the drying. You still wash by hand first. This is the step most people take.

**A full washer, {{price:62}} to £335.** Automates the washing too, which is the part you actually resent. This is the step most people wonder about and fewer take.

## Frequently asked

**Does an expensive washer sterilise better than boiling?** No. Boiling for ten minutes is on the NHS's own list of approved methods. A machine is faster, quieter and does not require you to stand there, but "better sterilised" is not a claim it can honestly make.

**Can I skip washing if the machine sterilises?** No, and this is the mistake worth avoiding. Milk residue has to come off before sterilising works properly, which is why a bottle washer washes first and why a steriliser expects clean bottles. The NHS is explicit that cleaning comes first.

**How long do I need to keep doing this?** Until at least 12 months, per NHS guidance. That is the number to run the maths on: a machine used twice a day for a year is doing roughly seven hundred cycles, which is a very different proposition from one bought for a newborn and abandoned at four months.

**Is a dishwasher enough?** A hot dishwasher cycle cleans, but the NHS lists sterilising as a separate step, so a dishwasher alone does not replace it for under-12-months.

**What happens to the machine afterwards?** Nothing, which is worth factoring in. A steriliser at {{price:86}} that becomes redundant after a year is a much easier loss to absorb than one at {{price:62}}.

## The bottom line

Buy the [steriliser](/product/86). For the overwhelming majority of households it is the right amount of machine, and the money saved is roughly a pram.

Buy the [washer](/product/62) only if you can name the number of bottles you wash a day and it is a high one. If you cannot name that number yet, you are not the buyer for it, and it will be cheaper to find out with a bottle brush first.
